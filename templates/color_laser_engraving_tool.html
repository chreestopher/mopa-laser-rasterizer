Why the settings come from you

MOPA laser color is a physical surface result, not a screen color sent directly to the machine. Power, speed, frequency, pulse duration, line interval, angle, focus, lens, material grade, finish, and preparation can all change the observed mark. The Rasterizer therefore uses settings you have tested and stored rather than inventing a universal recipe.

Start with MOPA color discovery, learn the role of pulse width and frequency, and keep repeatable recipes in a LightBurn Material Library.

Who this color engraving software is for

The workflow is designed for experienced LightBurn users who want to turn photographs, illustrations, logos, or abstract artwork into layered projects. It is also for people who are new to LightBurn—or have never used it—but are actively looking for a more capable alternative to the software supplied with their laser. Anyone interested in learning about or experimenting with MOPA laser color engraving can use these tools and guides as a practical starting point. MOPA fiber lasers offer the broadest parameter space for color marking on responsive metals, although every source and material must be tested independently.

The tool prepares files; it does not operate the laser, certify settings, or guarantee a physical color. Review laser compatibility and the MOPA laser workflow before production.

Color laser engraving tool questions

Does the Rasterizer generate MOPA color settings?

No. It maps artwork to settings in the Material Library you select. Those recipes should be developed and validated on the exact laser, lens, material, and focus used for production.

Does it work with LightBurn?

Yes. The workflow produces a .svg file and a .lbrn2 file intended for inspection and use in LightBurn.

Can it prepare a color photograph?

Yes. The Color Photo preset reduces the photograph to enabled palette colors and creates corresponding vector regions. Results depend on the available physical engraving palette.

Is colored engraving the same as printing ink?

No. Ink adds pigment on top of the metal. A laser instead heats or textures the surface to form oxide colors or optical interference patterns. Oxide develops from the metal's surface rather than being deposited on it, and the laser cannot independently control every part of that reaction. Laser-made palettes therefore tend to offer fewer reliably repeatable colors than ink, and some colors may change with the lighting or viewing angle.

Laser safety: Generated artwork and imported settings require operator review and material testing. Follow the machine and source manuals, use appropriate guarding and extraction, supervise operation, and maintain fire precautions.
